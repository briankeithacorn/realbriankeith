## Positioning & Separation from the Market

- The CAO audit is delivered by a **seven-figure business operator**, not a tech bro. Brian has run hiring/firing decisions, trademark issues, PR crises, P&L review at seven-figure companies. He also owns a non-AI company (Lenders Online Training) and builds AI systems himself.
- Most fractional CAIO / AI audit sellers come from pure technical or pure strategy backgrounds. Both share the same blind spot: they can't read a business.
- The QuickBooks screen-share move: ask the client to pull up books and walk through the ~100 most common AI app names; they tell you which ones they're actually paying for or have paid for. Starts with cash, not software. Nobody else does this.
- "I've had to include AI adoption in performance reviews." Not "I fired people" — the system framing is stronger. Approved AI use is a core job responsibility, not a personal choice. Typewriter-to-computer analogy: someone who brought a typewriter to work and refused to use a computer would just be done. Tie analogy: closer to "are you hitting your sales numbers" than "are you wearing a black or a red tie."
- Unapproved AI use creates real regulatory exposure, not just lost productivity. IBM 2026 breach report: shadow AI incidents more than doubled to 43%; 68% of breached organizations had no AI governance. EPC Group: average 67 unique AI tools per enterprise, 23% holding sensitive data.
- Investigation matters first: someone may have had 10 bad quality experiences with AI and is trying to do a good job. Two people in the same role can get wildly different results from the same task because one copied memory files into Claude instructions and the other forgot, or was out sick and buried in email three weeks later. AI access and setup should be treated like infrastructure outages, not casual preferences.
- Neutral third party: the CEO can't get honest answers from vendors who fix what they find, or from tech-side auditors without business experience.
- The audit finds what the client already suspects but can't name. What they know is broken is useful; what they suspect might be happening is the most interesting thing — and they cannot confirm it.
- "Most AI audits check your policies. Ours checks your books." (subhead framing; hero line is Brian's to write)
- Name: keep "AI audit" for honesty, lead with cash-flow angle so it doesn't sound like every other checklist.

## Brian's Definition of Context (verbatim, from Aurora)

Context = how people interact with AI. NOT generic "context vs content" (data vs documents). Context includes:
- What a human asked an AI
- What the AI answered
- Where the human put that information (email, Drive, Slack)
- What decisions the human made later about it (accessed again or never)
- How humans interact with other humans
- How AIs interact with other AIs

When AI becomes commoditized, context becomes the moat. Giving agents the right context eliminates 66% of hallucinations (IBM). The model is the commodity; what the model knows about your business is the only thing that can't be bought off the shelf.

## The C-Suite AI Usage Rubric (approved by Brian)

**Method:** Brian does NOT ask the executive what mode they're in. He asks questions to learn how they're using AI. After the 45-minute call, AI analyzes the transcript and suggests primary and secondary modes. Brian agrees or disagrees based on what he saw. After 45 minutes, you have a pretty good idea.

**Interview questions (per function):** "What are your handful of biggest job functions and how are you using AI across each of them?" People use one kind of AI over here, different over there, a lot over here, not much over there, never over there. AI is not a switch you turn on or off — it's a dial you turn up or down, but not across your entire role, across different parts of your role.

**Scoring:** primary bucket, secondary bucket, hours per week. Red flag: a leader using AI a couple hours a week while demanding 20 from the team.

**The ladder (six rungs, from research + Brian's naming):**
1. **The Googler** — looks things up a couple times a week. Brian's bucket. "I use it like Google."
2. **The Drafter** — writes emails, docs, summaries. Most common actual use (Gallup, Deloitte).
3. **The Consolidator** — feeds it data, gets it organized. 42% per Gallup.
4. **The Decision-Maker** — weighs options, assesses risk, reaches conclusions. Now the single biggest workplace use at 27.5% (Microsoft).
5. **The Thought Partner** — challenges the thesis, gives pushback, stress-tests judgment. Brian's bucket. Google: 72% of young leaders use it to ask questions they'd hesitate to ask a colleague.
6. **The Builder** — designs workflows, ships internal tools, runs agents. Geoffrey Litt's software-surgeon model. Rare at C-suite.

**High-level thinking (two categories Brian named):**
- **Orchestration:** chaining models — Claude to interview you and create a sales strategy, Grok for feedback, Gemini to find what others are saying. Using multiple AIs in concert (multimodal, copy-paste, building a harness).
- **Building:** "I built myself an app that did X, Y, Z because I needed it to be done."

**Low-level:** "I like to use ChatGPT because it helps me write emails better."

**Part of what we sell:** a neutral third party that helps the CEO properly assess executives — who is where, and what does that imply for future development. The CEO's constant job is not just recruitment but developing leaders. You can't mentor somebody if you don't know what the problem is. Easy to see a problem and say "what is that problem?" — then look deeper and find out the answer is "they're a Googler leading Builders."

**Open questions (unresolved, do not re-ask Brian):**

**Related research:** Slack's five personas measure adoption sentiment (Maximalist, Underground, Rebel, Superfan, Observer) — different axis from usage depth. The audit needs the depth axis.

## Executive Interviews (Stage 1 of 7)

- Stage one is reconnaissance, not confrontation. Lay the map of where the problem is. The second big chunk (after the intermediate report) is where you dig deeper, including using Brian's team for interviews.
- Brian interviews based on how he's feeling and where the conversation should go — not a rigid script of "these three things in this order."
- If he finds a soft spot in stage one and the person is in a critical role, he works around the issue until he confirms that's where they're defensive, then moves on. Point of stage one: map the problem.
- He might come back to a soft spot mid-interview if it feels appropriate.
- **Curiosity vs. defensiveness is the key tell.** A players get curious when pressed; C players get defensive. Most people don't lie — they exaggerate without understanding, overstate the case, cover over something uncomfortable, or don't go into as much detail as is important.
- When someone gives details Brian doesn't think are accurate, he keeps pressing right there. People almost never lie; they don't understand how important something is, and once you dial in ("well, what about this?"), they get curious.
- Brian's ability to not be BS'd: he's spent thousands of hours inside these apps, so he can hear the difference between someone describing a feature they read about and someone describing something they actually built. He's been in these roles since 2012 (started 2006 at an internet marketing agency).
- **Defensiveness handling depends on:** who's the person, their relationship to the CEO, what Brian already knows from other team members, how important they are to the company. If critical role, doesn't do good to piss them off.

## The Refusal Problem & Performance Reviews

- People refusing to use AI after counseling are more likely people who can't be reformed — they'll have to be let go or never advance again. Painful to have people in roles when they cannot actually advance.
- AI refusal is rarely isolated. It correlates with overall unwillingness to change workflows — e.g., refusing to record meetings ("I don't want AI stealing how I think").
- IP framing: whatever IP you create on the clock is the company's. That's what being an employee means — you get safety and security, you give up IP creation in most circumstances unless your contract has a specific carve out. If you don't want that, you should be an entrepreneur.
- Meeting recordings: no work calls were recorded a couple decades ago; now they almost all are. Context is critically valuable. If you say "I want to work as if it was the year 2000 and there's no note takers," you're saying you won't deliver the full value the company's paying for.
- Car wash analogy: if you work at a car wash and your job duty says sometimes you clean tires, and you say "I don't want to clean tires," that's a reason to counsel you — it's right there in the job description you signed. If you still refuse, you're fired. We'd be surprised if a car wash kept someone around who refused to clean tires. Same with Bob: if everybody records their calls but not Bob, disastrous cultural impact. If everybody puts data in the company cloud but Bob uses his own ChatGPT, people learn about it (they always will) and ask "why is Bob different?"
- **How Brian leads the counseling conversation:** appreciation first, not extractive. "We've really appreciated what you've done here, how long you've been here, you've done X, Y, Z." Then: "as we're getting new tool sets on board — Zoom recordings, Claude — here's why this matters. We're building a body of work." Example: a business coach who won't record coaching calls means the client doesn't have them (a problem), no quality control mechanism (a problem), and you can't understand how you actually win — you can only look at handwritten notes. The AI listening to everything is part of the modern tool set of understanding how you're successful.
- Job market: huge scarcity of talented people AND more applicants per posting than ever. Most opportunity in the future goes to people who embrace corporate AI rules. Like people willing to get on cell phones, use the internet, use fax machines instead of snail mail. Hire people who are AI-forward and willing to follow guidelines. If they're not willing, they're not our people.

## Hiring: AI Fluency Screening (every role, every stage)

- Screen at every level: resume, Indeed questions one by one, Wedge automated video interview, actual video/in-person interview. At every level, talking about AI, looking at how they answer.
- The way someone talks reveals a vast amount about how professional they really are.
- Core question at every stage: **"How did you use AI in the last seven days?"** Talk to me about that. Which tools? What tasks? What do you like it for? What do you not like it for? What's your favorite tool?
- The last-seven-days question can't be rehearsed. Someone who used AI yesterday can name the tool and the task; someone who hasn't can only talk in the abstract.
- Multi-stage approach catches different kinds of bluffs: resume catches the claim, automated video catches the unguarded answer, live interview catches whether they can talk about it like a professional.
- No duty to mentor or coach candidates — infinite number of candidates. Duty to mentor/coach starts when they become employees or contractors. Filter candidates for the very best in the skill set.
- Brian's read on weak answers: if they're already weak at AI this month/this year, they've announced they don't care about professional development. They want a job where they get paid to do nothing, or to do what was effective in 2015. They want a job in 2026? No — they want to be given money to not be productive.
- (Note: Brian's own earlier framing said investigation matters first for existing staff because of the relationship and mentoring duty — that logic does NOT apply to candidates.)

## Culture & Governance Thesis

- Enthusiastic AI use is wonderful. Enthusiastic use WITH a governance problem is fixable. The actual unfixable problem is people refusing to use the modern toolset.
- Proper AI audit doesn't just look at what tools are used by who — it looks at **behavior and culture**: who's using the tools, how does that relate to overall AI policies, who are the leaders controlling/enforcing/deploying/regulating AI policy inside the company, how robust is that, and does it map to the legal environment?
- Example of everything out of whack: great governance culture, great tool setup, but culture is "just use your own ChatGPT, no one's enforcing." Great regulations, compliance everywhere — but culture says use personal accounts, get it done yesterday. That "get the customer the end result yesterday" attitude is how a seven-figure company survives, and it can lead to incomplete AI policy following even when everyone's doing their best, because when people are overloaded they cut corners. Without robust AI policy/governance/culture/enforcement, the person gets the job done using whatever AI they need — high chance of shadow AI, context disappearing, regulatory exposure.
- **Executive AI use as a trust signal:** if a C-suite exec uses AI only a couple hours a week for lookups ("basically using it like Google") while asking the team to use AI across 20 hours a week, that's a red flag that at least that department doesn't have good AI use. It would be very odd to see a department with really strong AI use while the person running it uses AI as Google. Yellow-legal-pad boss analogy: if your boss is only taking notes on napkins and telling you to type up/voice-transcribe all your notes, why would you trust them as an expert?
- Measuring executive AI use should be part of the audit. The rubric (above) is the instrument.
